How trip planning works here

From “someday, maybe” to a guide in your pocket

Five skills, run in order. Each one takes the output of the last and makes it more real — an idea becomes a draft, a draft becomes reservations, reservations become a document, and the document becomes something you can actually use standing on a train platform.

You don't need to memorize any of this. You just need to know what the five stages are, what each one gives you, and the sentence that starts it.

The Map

Five stages, in order

Each stage is a separate skill with its own process and its own file. They're deliberately not one big automated pipeline — every handoff is a real stop where you decide whether to keep going. You can also jump in at any stage: if the trip is already booked, start at stage 4.

1

An interview that turns vague wants into a real, ranked list of 10–20 things.

2

Draft the trip

Shapes, dates, flights, lodging, activities, food — a rough plan with estimated prices.

3

Make it real

Re-prices everything live and stages each reservation up to the payment screen.

4

Write it up

A polished Word itinerary built from confirmation numbers, plus a packing list.

5

Put it in your pocket

A phone-friendly (or desktop) HTML guide that works offline during the trip.

The single most important thing: stages 2 through 5 need live search and file tools. Run them in Cowork, not plain Chat. A trip drafted in Chat and then checked in Cowork turned up eight errors nobody could have caught otherwise — flight costs 30–86% low, a hotel that had been sold and renamed two years earlier, and a museum visit that was physically impossible against the departure flight.
Stage 1

The Bucket List

bucket-list-builder ⇩ .zip

Most people's bucket list lives in their head as a handful of half-formed ideas. This stage is a structured interview that pulls it out and turns it into something you'll actually look at again.

What actually happens

  • Nine questions across three rounds — where you are in life, what past experiences stuck with you, and your real constraints (money, knees, who you'd go with, what you'd never do).
  • A picker round. You get slates of concrete options to react to — mostly clickable buttons, not typing. These aren't candidates; they're calibration, figuring out what kind of person you are about this stuff.
  • A sweep across the categories people forget: milestones, time with specific people, unfinished personal history, the one daring thing.
  • Slate and replace until you've got 10–20 items sorted into three horizons — Saturday-sized, this-year, and someday-big.
  • Top three get activated with a concrete, small first step each.

Why it's built this way

Asking "what's on your bucket list?" cold gets you three answers and a shrug. Reacting to specific options gets you a real profile, and the list built off that profile is one people keep rather than abandon.

How to kick it off

Just say what you want in plain language:

"Let's build my bucket list" "Help me figure out what I want to do before I die" "Let's update my bucket list" (annual refresh)

You can also build one for someone else as a gift — say so up front and it switches into proxy mode, asking for evidence about them rather than opinions.

You end up with

Bucket_List.md — the editable source Bucket_List.html — the rendered guide, with photos 45–75 min, conversational Cowork strongly preferred
The handoff: when an item graduates from "on the list" to "we're actually doing this," it becomes a trip. That's stage 2. It's offered, never automatic — and everything already learned about you (your reasons, your comfort floor, who you travel with, your vetoes) carries over so you don't get re-interviewed.
Stage 2

The Rough Plan

travel-planning-template ⇩ .zip

Seven phases that take a destination from "somewhere in Greece in September, maybe" to a complete written draft of the trip. Everything gets written into one running Master Trip Doc that grows phase by phase — not a new file each time.

The seven phases

  • 1–2 · Where & when. You get three distinct draft itineraries to choose between. If the destination is already settled, you get three different pacings of it instead — concentrated core vs. wider loop vs. slower and deeper.
  • 3 · Flights. Real routings and price ranges, both ends swept for alternate airports.
  • 4 · Lodging. Neighborhood-first, then specific properties.
  • 5 · Point-to-point travel. How you get between cities — with a simpler backup route noted alongside the optimized one.
  • 6 · What to do. Book-ahead items flagged with their release windows, plus flexible filler and an indoor/outdoor split for weather.
  • 7 · Where to eat. A working shortlist per city, not an encyclopedia.

How to kick it off

"Let's plan a trip to Portugal" "Help me plan our vacation" "I want to do 10 days in Japan next spring"

First thing it asks: whether to create a folder for the trip under your Travel project. Say yes — everything downstream expects it.

You end up with

Master Trip Doc — the running record Several sessions, days to weeks Cowork required from Phase 3 on
Nothing is booked here, and every price is a guess. A dry run found the rough draft's flight estimates 6–86% low and lodging 41–190% low. Treat the draft budget as directional and expect it to move when stage 3 checks live inventory. That's not a flaw in the draft — it's what the draft is for.
Stage 3

Booking

booking-playbook ⇩ .zip

This is where the plan meets reality. Every number from the draft gets re-priced live, every reservation gets set up and taken right to the payment screen — and then handed to you.

What actually happens

  • Pre-flight check first. Dates locked? Legal names as printed on the passports? Passport valid 3–6 months past your return? Visa or ETA needed? A failed check stops the process — a missed passport expiry is a blocking problem, not a footnote.
  • Everything re-priced before anything is booked, because draft numbers have already expired.
  • Booked in dependency order: flights → lodging → inter-city transport → timed-entry tickets → restaurants. Each step constrains the next, and the early ones are hardest to change. A tour ticket bought for a day your flight later invalidates is real money gone.
  • A conflict pass across the whole trip before anything gets committed — travel times that fit, venues open on the days they're scheduled.
  • A booking log tracking every line item's status, price, source, confirmation number, and cancellation deadline.

How to kick it off

"Let's book the Greece trip" "Price this out for real" "Stage these reservations"

Have passport details and loyalty numbers to hand before you start.

You end up with

Booking_Log.md — status, prices, confirmation #s Updated Master Trip Doc with real numbers Cowork only
The hard line: searching, pricing, comparing, and staging — yes. Entering payment details or completing a purchase — never. Every transaction is executed by you, on your own screen. What you get is a fully-loaded checkout page and a "go."
Stage 4

The Itinerary Document

itinerary-build-playbook ⇩ .zip

The formal write-up: a polished Word document built from confirmed logistics, not from memory. This is the version you print, email to the people coming with you, and hand to whoever's watching the house.

What actually happens

  • Hard facts gathered first, in order: uploaded docs, then Gmail confirmation emails, then Drive/project files, then whatever you say directly. Nothing is invented — a missing confirmation number becomes an open item, not a guess.
  • Structured to the trip: day-by-day for short trips or a single base hotel; block-by-block (one section per stay) for multi-city trips.
  • Opens with a "Trip at a Glance" table — every leg, its lodging, and whether it's Confirmed, Tentative, or Not booked.
  • A packing list built to the actual weather, activities, and dress codes of this trip.
  • Status tags throughout while it's a working draft — strippable on request for a clean "final" version.
  • A verification pass on anything time-sensitive: festival dates, transit schedules, whether a restaurant is still open.

How to kick it off

"Put together the itinerary doc" "Build the final trip document" "Write up the Munich itinerary"

This is the natural entry point if a trip is already booked and you just want it written up properly.

You end up with

Itinerary.docx (and .pdf) Packing list One session Chat or Cowork
Deliberately plain. No photos, no visual flourish — this stage is about accuracy and completeness. The good-looking version is stage 5, built from this document.
Stage 5

The HTML Guide

trip-html-guide ⇩ .zip

The finished itinerary becomes a real (tiny) website: plain HTML and CSS, no server, no app, works offline. Two formats, and you can have either or both.

 Mobile Pocket GuideDesktop Long-Scroll Guide
Shape Five linked pages with a fixed bottom tab bar, like an app One single scrolling file with a sticky in-page nav
Best for Using during the trip — today's plan, the hotel address, a confirmation number, standing at a platform Reading at a computer, sharing with everyone coming, printing to PDF
Pages / sections Home · Itinerary (one day at a time, with Previous/Next) · Travel · More · Files Hero → Overview → Itinerary → a deep-dive section for the trip's centerpiece → Travel → food, what to wear, useful phrases

Worth knowing

  • Both formats share one warm design language — parchment, navy, gold, oxblood — so a trip that ships both doesn't look like two unrelated products. The palette can be reskinned to the destination.
  • The Files page is one big tappable link to wherever your actual documents live (Drive, Dropbox, iCloud). Mark them available offline before you lose signal.
  • Every page is screenshotted at phone width before delivery, to catch layout breaks.
  • Open items get carried through as a visible checklist rather than quietly dropped.
  • If you want it on the web instead of in a file, it can be pushed to GitHub Pages as-is.

How to kick it off

"Make the pocket guide for Greece" "Turn the itinerary into an HTML guide" "Build me a phone version of this trip"

You'll be asked which format — mobile, desktop, or both — before anything is built. Don't skip the question; a three-day single-hotel trip rarely needs both.

You end up with

index.html + styles.css + pages (mobile) A single self-contained .html file (desktop) One session Cowork

Ground Rules

Things that hold across all five stages

Cowork, not Chat

From stage 2 onward, nearly every output is a factual claim about the world — a price, a schedule, a hotel's actual name today. Chat can't check any of them and will still answer confidently. Cowork can.

Every handoff is a real stop

Nothing chains automatically. At the end of each stage you're asked whether to start the next one. Say no and the work so far still stands on its own.

One folder per trip

Everything for a trip lives in its own subfolder under your Travel project — Greece/, Munich/. Each stage reads what the last one left there.

Nothing gets fabricated

A missing confirmation number, an unverifiable URL, an unknown price — these become flagged open items. They never become plausible-looking invented details.

You execute all payments

Reservations get staged right up to the payment screen and no further. Card details are never entered and purchases are never completed on your behalf.

Markdown is the source, HTML is the view

Where a stage produces both, edit the markdown — the HTML gets regenerated from it. Hand-editing the HTML means your change disappears at the next render.

Common Questions

If you're starting from nothing

Your situationWhere to start
No idea what I even want to doStage 1 — bucket-list-builder. Say "let's build my bucket list."
I know where I want to go, nothing plannedStage 2 — travel-planning-template. Say "let's plan a trip to ___."
I have a plan sketched out, ready to bookStage 3 — booking-playbook. Say "let's book this trip."
Everything's booked, I need it written upStage 4 — itinerary-build-playbook. Say "put together the itinerary doc."
I have an itinerary, I want it on my phoneStage 5 — trip-html-guide. Say "make the pocket guide."
My list is a year oldStage 1 refresh. Say "let's update my bucket list" — it re-scores what changed rather than starting over.
Do I have to say the skill name? No. Plain language works — the right skill gets picked up from what you're asking for. Naming it explicitly just removes any ambiguity, which is handy when two stages could both plausibly apply.
Can I skip a stage? Yes. The chain is a default, not a requirement. The one thing not worth skipping is stage 3's re-pricing — draft numbers are consistently and substantially low, and finding that out at checkout is worse than finding it out in a booking session.
What if I stop halfway? Every stage leaves a finished, standalone file behind. A rough plan with no bookings is still a useful rough plan. Pick it back up whenever — the next stage reads the folder, not your memory of the conversation.
